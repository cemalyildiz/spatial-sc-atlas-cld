What this is

A catalog of publicly deposited spatial transcriptomics and single-cell RNA-seq datasets, built for picking the right dataset rather than for reading abstracts. Records are harvested directly from NCBI GEO and EBI ArrayExpress, then classified so they can be filtered by how the data was produced and what it is about.

Which fields you can trust

Two kinds of field live on every record, and the difference matters when you cite something.

From the source database, verbatimDerived here, automatically
Accession · title · description · release date · PubMed ID · organism and sample count (GEO) Modality · platform · imaging vs sequencing · disease category · subtype · study type · tissue · cell count · organism (ArrayExpress, read from the text)

Derived fields are pattern-matched from each record's title and description. They are good enough to filter and browse with, and they are wrong often enough that you should open the source record — one click from every card — before a dataset goes into an analysis. Records carrying a Paired badge either mention both a spatial platform and single-cell data in their own text, or belong to a study that GEO split across several series where a sibling carries the other half. Those show a +1 series chip and list the sibling accessions in the detail panel — so a paired badge never means one accession necessarily holds everything. Confirm on the source page before relying on it.

Two disease rules are worth knowing because they move records between filters. A study whose title says it spans many cancer types is filed as pan-cancer even when its abstract names one cancer first — abstracts mention several cancers in passing far too often for that to be read as scope. And mesothelioma is its own category rather than a lung cancer, since it arises in the pleura or peritoneum; it no longer appears in lung filters.

More generally, the cancer named in the title wins over one met first in the abstract — an EGFR-inhibitor study of triple-negative breast cancer that cites lung cancer is a breast cancer record. The one exception is a title that only says "lung cancer" over an abstract that says NSCLC or SCLC: the more specific label is kept. When the text names no organ, the tissue is taken from the disease (glioma → brain, leukaemia → bone marrow / blood, melanoma → skin).

Modalities

Paired
Spatial and single-cell data referenced in the same record — the most useful kind for deconvolution, label transfer and spot annotation.
Spatial
Spatially resolved data only.
Single-cell
Dissociated single-cell or single-nucleus RNA-seq only. Cancer studies only, to keep the catalog focused.

Subtypes

A record can carry several subtype labels at once — a study can be both LUAD and EGFR-mutant — so the subtype filter matches any one of them rather than a single winner. Histological subtypes (LUAD, LUSC, LCNEC, invasive ductal and lobular, ESCC, ccRCC) and molecular ones (EGFR-mutant, ALK-rearranged, KRAS-mutant, IDH-mutant, HER2-positive, MSI-high, HPV-positive) live in the same list.

Short abbreviations are the danger here, so most are deliberately not matched on their own: ILC means innate lymphoid cell far more often than invasive lobular carcinoma, and IDC is also interdigitating dendritic cell. Those are detected from their full phrase only, which means a study that writes nothing but the abbreviation will carry no subtype — absence of a label is not evidence of absence. Subtypes are only assigned to records that already read as cancer.

Organ-specific subtypes must also fit the record's disease. A breast-cancer study whose abstract mentions lung adenocarcinoma in passing does not carry LUAD, so a LUAD search returns lung studies only. Molecular labels — EGFR- and KRAS-mutant, HER2-positive, IDH, MSI, HPV — occur in several cancers and are kept whatever the disease.

Study type

A small number of records are not biology but method or tool development: the deposit exists to demonstrate a new algorithm, and its data are a demonstration set chosen to make the method look good rather than to answer a biological question. They are labelled rather than dropped, since the data are often perfectly usable, and the Study type filter separates them. Such a record carries a Method chip.

A study is read as method development when its title follows the ACRONYM: … tool/framework/pipeline shape, when it begins with Benchmarking, or when the abstract says in so many words that the authors develop or present a computational method. That is a narrow test on purpose: a biological study that merely used a new tool should stay a biological study.

Studies split across several series

GEO often splits one study into a SuperSeries with a subseries per assay — the scRNA-seq half under one accession, the spatial half under another. Its API does not expose that link (the relations field comes back empty), but subseries reuse the study abstract verbatim, so records sharing an abstract are grouped here and treated as one study for pairing. That is why a record whose own data is single-cell can still be marked paired: its sibling holds the spatial half. The grouping is a heuristic on shared text, so check the siblings really are one study before building on it.

ArrayExpress splits studies too, but its companion records write their own abstract, so there is no shared text to group on. What they do share is the title once the assay words are stripped out — Single cell RNA-seq atlas of human NSCLC lesions and non-involved tissue beside Spatial transcriptomics (10x Visium) of human NSCLC lesions and non-involved tissue — and accession numbers submitted close together. Records matching on both are linked the same way. It is the looser of the two heuristics, so open the siblings before trusting a paired badge that rests on it.

Some GEO subseries do not share an abstract either — the snRNA-seq and Xenium halves of one osimertinib-resistance study (GSE292469, GSE342824) each describe their own assay. Their titles are identical apart from the bracketed assay tag at the end, so GEO records whose titles differ only in that tag are linked too.

That tag also says what each subseries actually holds. The shared abstract mentions every assay of the study, so on text alone a [scRNA-seq] subseries of a spatial study used to be listed with the spatial platform and marked paired on its own. Now a subseries tagged single-cell is single-cell (paired only through its sibling), one tagged spatial is spatial, and one tagged with an assay that is neither — bulk RNA-seq, ATAC (single-cell ATAC included), TCR/BCR repertoire, ChIP, CUT&Tag, methylation — is left out of the catalog, as is an untagged record whose title is about ATAC only. A plain [RNA-seq] inside a multi-series study is the bulk companion and is dropped, unless every other part of the study is a non-expression assay: GSE118389 [RNA-Seq] sits beside GSE118303 [WES], is the study's only expression data, and stays.

The tag and title also name the platform. The abstract of a multi-assay study lists every platform it used, so the platform a record's own title names wins: the [Visium] subseries of a study that also ran Xenium is a Visium record.

A related trap: many spatial studies never write "spatial transcriptomics" at all — they write ST and nothing else, which makes a spatial series look single-cell. ST is matched here only beside a word that removes the ambiguity (ST data, ST slides, a bracketed [ST] title), because a bare ST is far more often supratentorial ependymoma, ST-segment or the gene ST2.

Imaging-based vs sequencing-based

Imaging-based methods read a targeted gene panel in situ at single-cell or subcellular resolution — Xenium, MERFISH/MERSCOPE, CosMx, seqFISH, STARmap, in situ sequencing. Sequencing-based methods capture transcripts onto a barcoded surface and sequence them, giving whole-transcriptome coverage at spot, bead or region resolution — Visium and Visium HD, Slide-seq, Stereo-seq, GeoMx DSP, DBiT-seq. The trade-off is coverage against resolution, and it is usually the first thing you need to filter on.

Many spatial records never say which platform they used — they write "spatial transcriptomics" and stop. Those are listed as Spatial (unspecified) with production method Not stated instead of being counted as sequencing-based; the source record or the paper usually settles it.

How it stays current

A scheduled GitHub Action re-runs the harvest every Monday and commits the refreshed catalog straight to main, so the site tracks the source databases without anyone approving anything. The catalog is regenerated from scratch each run rather than appended to, and the run refuses to overwrite it if a source returns implausibly little, so a throttled database leaves the previous catalog standing. The harvest code is scripts/harvest.py — the classification vocabularies live at the top of that file and are meant to be edited.

Citing the data

This site is an index, not a source. Cite the original study and the accession, not this catalog. Check each record's terms of use before redistributing anything: a few carry controlled access even when the metadata is public.
